Essay title goes here: a clear, specific promise to the reader

A one or two sentence summary that sets up the question the essay explores and why it matters.

Jarrett MichaelSeptember 20, 2026·8 min read

Open with the sentence that earns the next one. The first paragraph is slightly larger so the reader knows where to begin.

Body paragraphs sit in a wide, comfortable reading column. Write the way you speak to a thoughtful friend: one idea per paragraph, concrete before abstract. Use links sparingly, and bold or italics only where the meaning needs it.1

A second paragraph continues the thought. When the argument turns, start a new section with a heading.

A section heading states the point of the section

Sections give long pieces a rhythm and let readers find their place again. Keep headings short and specific, and avoid clever ones that hide the point.

A quotation from another writer sits here, set apart with a quiet rule on the left.

Author name, Source title

After a quotation, say what it means. Do not let the quote do the interpretive work for you.

A smaller subheading for a sub-point

Use the smaller heading when a section has two or three distinct parts. A list works well for parallel items:

  • Each item is a full thought, not a fragment.
  • Keep the items parallel in structure.
  • Three to five items read best.

Scripture or source text

Set a primary text here, such as a passage of Scripture or an ancient source, so it stands apart from your own voice and can be read on its own.

Book 1:1–3

A single sentence worth remembering can be pulled out and set large, once or twice per essay.

Numbered steps are available too

  1. Name the problem plainly.
  2. Show why the obvious answer falls short.
  3. Offer a better one, and say what it costs.

Close with where you landed

End on the idea you want the reader to carry away, not on a summary. If there is a next step, name it.2
